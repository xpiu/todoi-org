How the scores work

Six criteria and one band, each from 1 to 5. The Score is the unweighted mean of those seven. Two of the six criteria differ between the directories.

The score

The number is an editorial summary. The reasoning, written on each record, is the point.

The Score column is the unweighted mean of all seven, so the band carries one seventh of it. Scores are editorial judgements on a 1-5 scale.

Confidence is marked on every record. Each directory defines High, Medium-high and Medium in its own words, under its own criteria.

Open-source directory

Six criteria and the community band, each 1 to 5, each with a written justification, on the open‑source directory.

Ease of deployment
Containers and packages, dependency count, operational complexity, upgrade burden and the state of the documentation.
Ease of use
Interface clarity, learning curve, conceptual overhead and whether non-specialists can live in it.
Enterprise features
Permissions, identity, audit and governance, portfolio reporting, scalability and administration.
Interface flexibility
How many genuinely useful views exist, plus workflow and custom-field options, dashboards, filters and extension points.
Data sovereignty
Whether you can run it independently, read its source, and keep control of the application and its data infrastructure.
Portability
APIs and exports, conventional storage and database choices, licensing constraints, and how realistically you could leave.
Community size
The community band read as a score, from Small at 1 to Very large at 5. A project more people run, extend and answer questions about is easier to get help with and safer to depend on.

The open-source directory opens by community size, largest first. Each record also carries a Confidence value: High is stronger evidence and a second reading; Medium-high flags a remaining qualification; Medium is usually a first pass. Read the caveat and sources with the label.

Every record carries a Source map: the app, the server, the paid extras and the parts published separately, each marked open, partly open or closed, with the licence named. FOSS means app and server are open and no closed code is held back; open core means the paid extras are closed; source-available means the code can be read under restrictions; mixed source means app and server differ. The four labels, how a paid feature sits beside each of them, and a way to choose a licence from all rights reserved to the public domain, are on the licensing page.

Commercial directory

Six criteria and the market band, each 1 to 5, each with a written justification, on the commercial directory. Four criteria are shared with the open-source directory; two change, because a service someone else runs raises different questions.

Value for money
What the free plan gives, what the tier most teams actually need costs per seat, seat minimums, and what is held back for the top tiers or sold as an add-on.
Ease of use
Interface clarity, learning curve, conceptual overhead and whether non-specialists can live in it.
Enterprise features
SSO and SCIM, permissions, audit logs, admin controls, compliance certifications, portfolio reporting and scale.
Interface flexibility
How many genuinely useful views exist, plus custom fields, automation, dashboards, filters and extension points.
Data control
Where the data lives and who decides: region choice, certifications, encryption key management, a self-managed edition, and what AI features do with your content.
Portability
Export formats and how complete they are, the public API, importers, and how realistically you could leave.
Market presence
The market band read as a score, from Small at 1 to Very large at 5. A product more organisations pay for is likelier to stay supported, keep its integrations current and still be sold in five years.

The commercial directory opens by market presence, largest first. Each record also carries a Confidence value: High means pricing, features and data handling were verified on the vendor’s own current pages; Medium-high flags one remaining qualification; Medium means part of the record rests on secondary sources. Read the caveat and sources with the label.

A product sold only by quote scores low on value for money by design: a price a reader cannot see cannot be judged good value. That pulls the mean of enterprise suites down, so read their other five criteria on their own.

The two directories share a scale but not two of their criteria, so a commercial product and an open-source one are best compared on the four criteria they share, not on the mean.

Every record also carries a Source map: the app, the server, the paid extras and the parts the owner publishes separately, each marked open, partly open or closed. Everything in the commercial directory has a closed app and server. Closed core marks the products whose owner publishes a working part of the product as open source, such as an editor or a UI kit, and has updated it in the last twelve months; API clients and other tooling alone do not count. It is the mirror image of open core.

Who each product is built for

The label follows the market the vendor sells to, not every use the product can be put to. Plenty of software teams run on a general team tool, and plenty of individuals live in one built for teams.

Personal
Sold to individuals first: one person’s tasks, priorities and days. Some allow sharing, but there is little or no team administration, which is why the enterprise score is low by design.
Teams
General work management for any team: projects, assignments and shared views, sold per seat to small companies and departments as much as to large organisations.
Software
Issue tracking and delivery for software teams: backlogs, sprints, releases and links to code. Other teams can use it, but it is built around engineering’s vocabulary.
Enterprise
Portfolio, resource and professional-services management sold mainly to large organisations, often through a sales conversation rather than a self-serve plan.
